## Why this matters for a PM

Interviewing the wrong people is worse than interviewing no one: you get confident conclusions about the wrong users. Recruiting is where most beginner research quietly fails. A good screener and a smart outreach plan get you 5–8 of the right people in a few days, for free.

## Core concepts

### Who you need: define the participant

Before recruiting, write a **participant profile**: the exact kind of person whose behaviour you want to understand. It has three parts:

1. **Must-have behaviour.** What they’ve actually done recently. “Ordered groceries online at least twice in the last month.” Behaviour beats demographics.
2. **Context.** Where and how. “Lives in Bengaluru, shares a flat with at least one person.”
3. **Exclusions.** Who to leave out. People who work in the industry (they think like insiders), close friends (too polite), people who’ve never done the behaviour.

Demographics (age, gender, income) matter only when they change behaviour. “Age 22–30” is often a lazy stand-in for “recently started working and manages their own money”. Say the behaviour directly.

### What a screener is

A **screener** is a short questionnaire that filters people before you invite them. It checks they match the profile without revealing what you’re looking for. If people can guess the “right” answers, some will, because they want the gift card or want to help.

Good screener questions:
- **Ask about behaviour with ranges**: “In the last 30 days, how many times did you order groceries online? 0 / 1 / 2–4 / 5+.” You want 2–4 or 5+.
- **Hide the target**: mix the real question with others. “Which of these apps have you used in the last month? (Swiggy, Zepto, BigBasket, Amazon, Dunzo, None).”
- **Include an open text question**: “Describe the last time you shared an expense with someone.” It filters people who can’t articulate anything and reveals great interviewees.
- **Capture logistics**: name, contact, city, availability, and consent to be contacted.

Keep it under 2 minutes: 5–8 questions.

### Where to find participants (free)

- **Your network, one step removed:** ask friends to forward your screener to *their* friends. Second-degree contacts are honest but reachable.
- **LinkedIn posts:** “I’m researching how flatmates manage shared expenses. If you live with flatmates in Bengaluru, I’d love 30 minutes. Form here.” Posts from people learning in public get good responses.
- **WhatsApp and Telegram groups:** housing groups, alumni groups, apartment groups (ask the admin first).
- **Reddit:** r/bangalore and niche subreddits (follow the rules on self-promotion).
- **College and community Discords/Slack groups.**
- **In-person (“guerrilla”) research:** short conversations at a café, a co-working space or outside a store. Great for quick, broad input.
- **Product communities:** users who write reviews or post complaints on X are motivated and articulate. Reply politely and ask for a chat.

### Incentives

Paid research uses gift cards (₹300–₹1,000 for 30 minutes is common in India). On a zero budget, offer: sharing your findings with them, a heartfelt thank-you, a LinkedIn recommendation for their help, or a small ₹100–₹200 UPI thank-you if you can afford it. Be honest that it’s a learning project.

### Bias in recruiting

- **Convenience bias:** you talk only to people who are easy to reach (friends, classmates), who may not represent your users.
- **Self-selection bias:** people who volunteer may be more enthusiastic or more frustrated than average.
- **Survivorship bias:** you talk only to current users and miss the people who tried and left. Former users often explain the biggest problems. Recruit some on purpose.

## Worked example

**P1 topic:** “How young professionals in Bengaluru manage monthly shared household expenses.”

**Participant profile:**
- Must-have behaviour: shared at least 3 household expenses (rent, utilities, groceries, cook/maid) with a flatmate or partner in the last 2 months.
- Context: lives in Bengaluru; working full time.
- Exclusions: works at a fintech or expense-splitting company; close friends of the researcher.
- Aim: 6 people. Include at least 2 who tried an app like Splitwise and stopped.

**Screener (Google Form):**
1. Which city do you live in? (Bengaluru / Other)
2. Who do you live with? (Alone / Flatmates / Partner / Family / Other)
3. In the last 2 months, which of these have you paid for and shared the cost of with someone? (Rent, Electricity, Wi-Fi, Groceries, Cook/maid, Subscriptions, None)
4. Which apps or methods have you used to track shared expenses? (WhatsApp messages, Notes app, Excel/Sheets, Splitwise, Nothing in particular, Other)
5. Describe the last time you settled up shared expenses. What happened? (Open text)
6. Do you work in fintech or at a company that makes expense apps? (Yes/No)
7. Would you be open to a 30-minute video call this week? (Yes/No) + preferred times
8. Name, email or phone (for scheduling only)

**Selection:** From 23 responses, 14 qualify (Bengaluru, flatmates/partner, 3+ expenses, not in fintech). Pick 6 with the richest answers to question 5, including 2 former Splitwise users.

## Step by step

1. **Write the participant profile**: must-have behaviour, context, exclusions, target number (6–8), and any mix you need (e.g., 2 former users).
2. **Build the screener** in Google Forms (see the tool guide). 5–8 questions, behaviour ranges, one open question, logistics last.
3. **Test it** with one friend: does it take under 2 minutes? Can they guess what you’re looking for?
4. **Write a short outreach message**: who you are, what you’re learning, time needed, what they get, link.
5. **Post in 4–5 channels** on Monday: LinkedIn, two WhatsApp groups (with permission), Reddit, and ask 5 friends to forward.
6. **Review responses daily.** Mark qualified people in the response sheet.
7. **Invite the best fits** with 3 time options. Send a calendar invite with a Google Meet link.
8. **Send a reminder** 2 hours before. Expect 1 in 5 to cancel; over-book by 1–2.
9. **Thank everyone**, including people you didn’t pick.

## Common mistakes

1. **Recruiting by demographics only.** “Women, 25–35” tells you nothing about behaviour.
2. **Obvious screeners.** “Do you find splitting expenses frustrating? Yes/No” invites people to say yes.
3. **Only talking to current, happy users.** Former users and non-users explain churn and adoption barriers.
4. **Recruiting too late.** Start recruiting on Monday for interviews Wednesday–Friday.
5. **Interviewing friends as real data.** Use them to pilot the guide, not as evidence.
6. **Forgetting consent and privacy.** Collect only what you need, and tell people how you’ll use it.

## How it shows up in interviews

> [!INTERVIEW]
> **“How would you find users to talk to for a new B2B product?”**
> Outline: define the role and behaviour (e.g., finance managers at 50–200 person companies who reconcile payments monthly) → channels (LinkedIn search and outreach, communities, customer success team’s contacts, lost deals) → screener on role and behaviour → incentive (findings report, gift card) → aim for 5–8 per segment.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> **“How do you avoid bias in user research?”**
> Outline: recruit by behaviour, not convenience → include former and non-users → hide the target in screeners → ask about past behaviour, not opinions → separate observations from interpretations → look for disconfirming evidence.

1. A participant profile (must-have behaviour, context, exclusions, number, mix).
2. A 7-question screener.
3. A 4-line LinkedIn outreach post.

```answer Reveal a model answer
**Profile**
- Must-have behaviour: made their first mutual fund investment (SIP or lump sum) within the last 24 months.
- Context: lives in an Indian metro; salaried.
- Exclusions: works in finance/wealth management or at an investing app; close friends.
- Number: 6–7. Mix: at least 2 who started and then stopped an SIP; at least 2 who used an app (Groww, Zerodha Coin, Paytm Money) and 1 who used a bank or advisor.

7. Available for a 30-minute video call this week? + Name and contact

**LinkedIn post**

```

## Quiz

```quiz
Q: What should a participant profile focus on first?
- [ ] Age and gender :: Demographics matter only when they change behaviour.
- [x] Recent behaviour relevant to the problem :: Correct. “Did X in the last 30 days” predicts relevance far better than demographics.
- [ ] Their opinion of your product idea :: Opinions about your idea aren’t a recruiting criterion.
- [ ] Their income level :: Only relevant if it affects the behaviour you’re studying.

Q: Which screener question is best?
- [ ] Do you find managing money stressful? (Yes/No) :: Obvious and leading; people will say yes.
- [x] In the last 30 days, how many times did you transfer money to a flatmate? (0 / 1 / 2–4 / 5+) :: Correct. Behavioural, specific, and hard to game.
- [ ] Would you use an expense-splitting app? :: Asks about the future and reveals your target.
- [ ] Are you a good fit for this study? :: Invites everyone to say yes.

Q: Why include former users in your research?
- [ ] To win them back during the interview :: Interviews aren’t sales.
- [x] They often explain the biggest problems and the reasons people leave :: Correct. Ignoring them is survivorship bias.
- [ ] They are easier to recruit :: Usually they’re harder to reach.
- [ ] They give higher satisfaction scores :: They usually give lower ones, which is exactly why they’re useful.

Q: You need 6 completed interviews. Roughly how many people should your outreach reach?
- [ ] 6 :: Many won’t respond, qualify or show up.
- [ ] 15 :: Usually too few once you account for drop-off.
- [x] Around 150–200 :: Correct. Drop-off happens at every step: response, qualification, scheduling, attendance.
- [ ] 5,000 :: Far more than needed for a small qualitative study.

Q: What is the main purpose of an open-text question in a screener?
- [ ] To make the form longer :: Length isn’t a goal.
- [x] To filter out people who can’t describe their experience and spot articulate interviewees :: Correct. It reveals who has a real, specific story.
- [ ] To collect quotes for your final report :: Screener answers can hint at themes, but report quotes should come from interviews.
- [ ] To check spelling :: Not relevant.

Q: Which is an example of convenience bias?
- [x] Interviewing only your classmates because they’re easy to reach :: Correct. They may not represent your actual users.
- [ ] Using a screener to filter by behaviour :: That reduces bias.
- [ ] Including two former users on purpose :: That reduces survivorship bias.
- [ ] Asking about the last time something happened :: That’s good interview technique, not a recruiting bias.
```

## Key takeaways

- Write a **participant profile** based on **recent behaviour**, context and exclusions.
- A **screener** filters people without revealing the “right” answers; use ranges and one open question.
- Recruit through **second-degree networks**, LinkedIn, communities and in-person chats; reach **~200 people for ~6 interviews**.
- Deliberately include **former users and non-users** to avoid survivorship bias.
- Start recruiting **Monday**, over-book by 1–2, and always thank participants.
