## Why this matters for a PM

“Product manager” means very different days at a 30-person startup and at Google. Knowing the difference helps you choose where to apply, tailor your story, and answer “Why this company?” convincingly. In Bengaluru, you’ll apply to both kinds, often in the same week.

## Core concepts

### Company stages, briefly

- **Early-stage startup** (seed to Series A, roughly 10–80 people): finding product–market fit. Examples: many AI startups in HSR Layout and Koramangala.
- **Growth-stage startup** (Series B to D, roughly 100–2,000 people): scaling what works. Examples: Zepto, CRED, Groww in their scaling years.
- **Large tech company / big tech** (thousands of people, often public or very large): many mature products, global scale. Examples in Bengaluru: Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Flipkart, Walmart Global Tech, Atlassian, Uber.

### How the PM job differs

| Dimension | Early-stage startup | Growth-stage | Big tech |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scope | Broad: the whole product, often | A feature area (e.g., checkout) | Narrow but deep: one part of a huge product |
| Speed | Ship in days | Ship in weeks | Ship in weeks to months; more reviews |
| Data | Little data, lots of intuition and interviews | Good data, experimentation starting | Huge data, strong experimentation culture |
| Process | Light; you invent it | Forming; you help shape it | Established: planning cycles, design reviews, launch checklists |
| Who you work with | Founders directly | Eng/design leads, a few stakeholder teams | Many teams, legal, privacy, marketing, other PMs |
| What “impact” looks like | Did we find users who love it? | Did we grow metric X by Y%? | Did we move a metric by 0.5% for 100M users? |
| Mentorship | Little formal training | Some | Strong: APM programs, peer reviews, career ladders |
| Risk | Company might fail; role can change overnight | Moderate | Low; stable |
| Pay mix in India | Lower cash, more ESOPs (stock options) | Mixed | Higher cash + stock (RSUs) |

Two terms:
- **ESOPs (employee stock options):** the right to buy company shares later at a fixed price. Valuable only if the company does well and shares can be sold.
- **RSUs (restricted stock units):** actual shares granted over time, common at large public companies. More predictable value.

### What each environment rewards

**Startups reward:** bias to action, comfort with ambiguity, doing many jobs (some design, some analytics, some customer support), talking to users constantly, and writing just enough documentation.

**Big tech rewards:** structured thinking, clear writing (long docs are read and reviewed), stakeholder management across many teams, data-driven decisions with experiments, and patience with process.

**Both reward:** customer focus, clear communication, owning outcomes.

### Which is better for a first PM role?

Neither is strictly better. A rough guide:

- Choose **big tech or a structured APM program** if you want training, a strong brand on your resume, and clear feedback loops. The trade-off: smaller scope and slower pace.
- Choose a **growth-stage startup** if you want real ownership quickly with some structure. Many first-time PMs thrive here.
- Choose an **early-stage startup** if you’re comfortable figuring things out alone and the founders are strong mentors. The trade-off: you may learn bad habits without guidance.

For you specifically: your technical, customer-facing background is valued at **B2B SaaS and AI companies** of all sizes, where explaining technical products to customers is daily work.

### How to tell what a company is really like

Job descriptions use the same words everywhere. Look for signals:
- Number of PMs and engineers (LinkedIn “People” tab).
- Funding stage and date of last round (news, Tracxn, Crunchbase).
- Whether they mention experimentation, OKRs, design reviews (more structure).
- Whether the role reports to a founder (startup) or a Group PM (larger company).

## Worked example

**Same problem, two companies: “Users drop off during sign-up.”**

**At a 25-person fintech startup:**
- Monday: the PM looks at the few hundred sign-ups from last week in a spreadsheet and calls 6 users who dropped off.
- Tuesday: finds that the PAN verification step fails often on one Android version. Writes a short note in Slack with screenshots.
- Wednesday: sketches a fix with the one designer, agrees with the two engineers to ship a workaround.
- Friday: fix is live. The PM watches sign-up completion daily.
- Documentation: a half-page note. Decision-maker: the founder, in a 10-minute chat.

**At a big tech payments product with 50M users:**
- Week 1: the PM pulls funnel data by device, OS, region and app version from the data warehouse. Finds a 1.8% drop at PAN verification on certain devices.
- Week 2: writes a 4-page doc: problem, data, options, risks, experiment plan. Reviews with engineering, design, data science, the identity platform team and compliance.
- Week 3–4: the identity platform team schedules the fix in their sprint. An A/B test is set up with guardrails on fraud.
- Week 6: experiment reads out: +1.2% sign-up completion, no fraud increase. Gradual rollout to 100%.
- Impact: 1.2% of a huge number is hundreds of thousands of users a month.

Same skill set, very different rhythm.

## Step by step

How to tailor yourself to a company type:

1. **Identify the stage** using headcount, funding and the job description.
2. **List what that stage rewards** (see the table above).
3. **Pick two stories from your past** that show those traits. For a startup: a time you figured something out alone and shipped fast. For big tech: a time you aligned many people with a clear written proposal.
4. **Adjust your language.** Startups: “talked to customers, shipped, iterated”. Big tech: “wrote a proposal, aligned stakeholders, ran an experiment, measured impact at scale”.
5. **Prepare your “Why this company?” answer** around their stage’s challenge. Example for a growth startup: “You’re scaling from metro cities to tier 2. I want to work on making onboarding work for very different users.”
6. **Ask a stage-appropriate question** at the end of interviews. Startup: “What would make this role a failure in six months?” Big tech: “How does the team decide what goes into the next planning cycle?”

## Common mistakes

1. **Telling a startup you love process.** They hear “slow”. Talk about speed and ownership instead.
2. **Telling big tech you hate process.** They hear “won’t work well with 12 teams”. Talk about clear writing and alignment.
3. **Assuming a big brand means a better learning curve.** Some big-tech PM roles are narrow. Ask about scope.
4. **Ignoring ESOP terms.** At startups, ask about vesting schedule and how recent the valuation is before treating ESOPs as cash.
5. **Using one generic “Why product?” answer everywhere.** Tailor the second half to the company’s stage.

## How it shows up in interviews

> [!INTERVIEW]
> **“Why do you want to work at a startup (or at our company)?”**
> Outline: their stage and challenge → what you want to learn → two concrete strengths that fit that stage → one sentence about their product you genuinely admire or would improve.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> **“How would your approach change if you had 10x fewer resources?”**
> Outline: narrower scope → fewer, cheaper tests (interviews, fake doors, manual work behind the scenes) → faster decisions with less data → be explicit about risks you’re accepting.

- [ ] Startups don’t test anything :: Good startups test, just more lightly.

## Key takeaways

- The PM title covers very different jobs at **early-stage, growth-stage and big tech** companies.
- Startups reward **speed, ambiguity and breadth**; big tech rewards **structure, writing and scale**.
- Read the stage from **headcount, funding and the job description**, then tailor your stories.
- Understand **ESOPs vs. RSUs** before comparing offers.
- Your technical, customer-facing background is a strong fit for **B2B, AI and platform** roles at any size.
