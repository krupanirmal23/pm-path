## Why this matters for a PM

Vision, strategy and goals are three of the most misused words in companies. When they’re confused, teams chase whatever the loudest person wants. When they’re clear, every prioritisation decision gets easier because you can ask, “Does this serve our strategy?” Your P1 strategy doc this week needs all three.

## Core concepts

### Vision: where we’re going

A **product vision** describes the future you want to create for users, usually 3–10 years out. It’s inspiring and stable. It answers: *What will the world look like for our users if we succeed?*

Good visions are:
- **User-centred:** about the change in people’s lives, not about the company.
- **Ambitious but understandable:** a 12-year-old could repeat it.
- **Stable:** it doesn’t change every quarter.

Example (illustrative, for a grocery app): “Every household in urban India gets what it needs, when it needs it, without planning.”

A vision is not a strategy. It tells you the destination, not the route.

### Strategy: how we’ll win

A **product strategy** is a set of choices about how you’ll reach the vision given your situation: which users to serve first, which problems to focus on, what makes you different, and what you won’t do. Strategy is about **choices and trade-offs**. If a “strategy” doesn’t rule anything out, it isn’t one.

A simple structure for strategy (adapted from common strategy practice):
1. **Diagnosis:** what’s going on? The key challenge or opportunity, based on evidence.
2. **Guiding policy:** the overall approach to deal with that challenge.
3. **Coherent actions:** a few focused bets that support the policy and reinforce each other.

Another useful lens is to answer five questions:
- **Where will we play?** Which users, needs, markets.
- **How will we win?** Why users will choose us over alternatives (our advantage).
- **What capabilities do we need?** Skills, tech, data, partners.
- **What won’t we do?** Explicit no’s.
- **How will we know it’s working?** The goals.

### Goals: how we’ll measure progress

**Goals** are measurable targets for a specific time period, usually a quarter or a year, that show whether the strategy is working. They are the most concrete layer. Next lesson covers **OKRs**, a popular goal-setting format.

Good goals are outcome-based (from Week 1): “Increase weekly repeat buyers in Pune from 18% to 25% by June,” not “launch in Pune”.

### How they fit together

| Layer | Question | Time horizon | Changes | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mission | Why do we exist? | Forever | Rarely | “Make commerce accessible to every Indian.” |
| Vision | What future are we creating? | 3–10 years | Rarely | “Every small seller can reach buyers across India from their phone.” |
| Strategy | How will we win, and what won’t we do? | 1–3 years | Yearly-ish | “Win first-time online sellers in tier 2–3 cities with the simplest, zero-commission selling in regional languages; don’t chase premium brands.” |
| Goals / OKRs | How do we measure progress now? | Quarter / year | Every quarter | “New sellers listing 10+ products in their first week: 30% → 45%.” |
| Roadmap | What will we work on, in what order? | Weeks to months | Often | “Now: bulk listing from photos. Next: regional language support.” |

Each layer should explain the one below it. If you can’t connect a roadmap item to a goal, a strategy choice and the vision, question the item.

### Signs of a weak strategy

- **A list of goals pretending to be a strategy.** “Grow 3x, improve NPS, launch AI” are targets, not choices.
- **Everything is a priority.** No trade-offs means no strategy.
- **No diagnosis.** If it doesn’t say what the core challenge is, it’s a wish.
- **Copying competitors.** “Do what Blinkit does, but better” isn’t a reason customers will switch.

## Worked example

**Meesho (illustrative, based on public information).**

- **Vision:** Make e-commerce accessible to the next set of Indian consumers and sellers, especially beyond big cities.
- **Diagnosis:** Large marketplaces were built around metro shoppers and established brands. Hundreds of millions of value-conscious buyers in smaller towns and millions of small sellers were underserved: products felt expensive, apps felt complex, and sellers faced high commissions.
- **Guiding policy:** Be the lowest-cost, simplest marketplace for unbranded, value products, focused on tier 2+ India.
- **Coherent actions:**
  - Zero commission for sellers, to grow supply of low-priced products.
  - A simple app in many Indian languages.
  - A logistics model optimised for low cost rather than fastest delivery.
- **What they chose not to do:** compete for premium brands and same-day delivery in metros.
- **Goals (example):** increase repeat purchase rate among tier 3 buyers; increase sellers who stay active after 90 days.

Notice how the “won’t do” makes the strategy real. Fast delivery is valuable, but chasing it would raise costs and break the value proposition.

## Step by step

Writing the vision and strategy sections of your P1 strategy doc:

1. **Write the vision** in one sentence: the change in your users’ lives if P1 succeeds in 3–5 years.
2. **Write the diagnosis** (3–5 sentences) from your Week 2 research and Week 3 competitor work: what’s the core challenge or opportunity?
3. **Answer “where will we play?”**: your primary persona and their main job.
4. **Answer “how will we win?”**: your advantage. What will you do that alternatives don’t, and why can’t they easily copy it?
5. **List 2–3 coherent actions** (big bets) that reinforce each other.
6. **Write “what we won’t do”**: at least 3 explicit no’s, each with a reason.
7. **Set 1–2 goals** (outcome-based, measurable) to check the strategy is working. Next lesson turns these into OKRs.
8. **Check the chain**: does every bet serve the diagnosis? Would a competitor’s strategy doc look identical? If so, sharpen it.

## Common mistakes

1. **Vision about the company, not users.** “Become India’s #1 app” isn’t a vision users care about.
2. **Strategy without trade-offs.** If nothing is ruled out, it’s a wish list.
3. **Goals that are outputs.** “Launch 5 features” doesn’t measure strategy.
4. **Skipping the diagnosis.** Without naming the challenge, actions are random.
5. **Changing the vision every quarter.** That signals there was never a vision.
6. **Ignoring the competition.** “How will we win?” needs a reason customers will pick you.

## How it shows up in interviews

> [!INTERVIEW]
> **“What’s the difference between vision and strategy?”**
> Outline: vision = future you’re creating for users (long-term, stable); strategy = choices about how to get there given your situation, including what you won’t do; goals measure progress. Use one company example across all three.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> **“What should Swiggy’s strategy be for the next two years?”**
> Outline: diagnosis (mature food delivery, intense quick-commerce competition, profitability pressure) → where to play (e.g., high-frequency urban households) → how to win (e.g., one membership across food and groceries, reliability) → 2–3 coherent bets → what not to do → goals.

## Practice exercise

**Task (35 minutes):** Write a one-page vision and strategy for **CRED** as if you were its head of product. Include vision, diagnosis, where to play, how to win, 3 coherent actions, 3 things you won’t do, and 2 goals.

```answer Reveal a model answer (illustrative)
**Vision:** Financially responsible people in India are recognised and rewarded for good financial behaviour, and their money life is effortless.

**Diagnosis:** CRED built a large base of high-credit-score credit card users through bill payments and rewards. Paying bills is a low-margin, low-frequency activity. The challenge: turn trusted, affluent users into a profitable relationship without diluting the premium, trustworthy brand.

**Where to play:** Urban professionals with good credit and credit cards, who value convenience and status.

**How to win:** Deep trust and a premium experience with a curated, creditworthy user base, which lets CRED offer better financial products (credit, payments) with lower risk than general apps.

**Coherent actions:**
1. Make CRED the default place to pay for the member’s regular financial obligations (cards, rent, utilities, UPI), increasing frequency.
2. Offer selective credit products to members, priced using CRED’s understanding of their financial behaviour.
3. Use rewards that reinforce good behaviour (on-time payments), not just discounts.

**Won’t do:** chase mass-market users with low credit scores; compete on cashback with mass payment apps; launch products that dilute the premium feel.

**Goals:** Monthly transacting members per active member: from X to Y. Share of members using at least two CRED financial products: from A% to B%.
```

## Key takeaways

- **Vision** = the future you create for users; **strategy** = choices on how to win; **goals** = measurable progress.
- A real strategy includes a **diagnosis**, a **guiding policy**, **coherent actions** and explicit **no’s**.
- Answer **where to play, how to win, what capabilities, what we won’t do, how we’ll know**.
- Each layer should **explain the one below**; roadmap items must trace to goals and strategy.
- If a strategy **rules nothing out**, it isn’t a strategy.
